Comparing fractions

Two fractions, compared as areas of squares of the same size. Each square is divided into as many parts as its denominator and the numerator is filled in; at the end both are cut into matching parts and counted. Cutting to match never moves the filled area.

Central question
Which is larger, 2/3 or 3/5? How can you tell?
Assumed background
You have seen fractions written as a/b. You have met the idea of one part of a whole divided equally. You can compare areas by eye.
Written for
Upper primary to lower secondary

Six steps in all: divide, fill, divide, fill, cut to match, count.

The two squares are always the same size. Thick lines are the original division (the denominator); thin lines are the extra cuts that make the two match. The filled area stays where it was — the line weights let you follow that. The result also appears as the sign in the middle (> < =) and in the table below.

Current state

What you can do here

  • Change the numerator alone, or the denominator alone, and compare how the area changes. A bigger numerator does not always mean a bigger fraction.
  • Watch the "cut to match" step one move at a time: the parts get finer while the filled area stays put.
  • Enter pairs like 1/2 and 2/4 that are written differently but the same size, and confirm it by area and by the = sign.

The denominator says how many parts the whole was cut into; the numerator says how many of them were taken. Start from the fact that the two were cut differently.

While the cuts differ, counting pieces against pieces answers nothing. Make the cuts match first, then count.

Thirds and fifths can both be cut into fifteenths: 2/3 is 10/15 and 3/5 is 9/15.

In more detail
Wording

Picture two cakes of the same size, one cut into 3 and the other into 5. A slice from one is not the same as a slice from the other, so counting slices settles nothing. Cut both into 15 and every slice is the same size — now the counts can be compared.

Take L, the least common multiple of the denominators, and rewrite a/b = (a × L/b)/L and c/d = (c × L/d)/L. The unit fraction 1/L is now shared, so comparing numerators compares the values. For 2/3 and 3/5, L = 15 gives 10/15 and 9/15, hence 2/3 > 3/5.

For teachers

A common misreading: deciding that the bigger numerator (or the bigger denominator) wins. Here the numerator and the denominator move independently, so either can be tested on its own. Raising only the denominator visibly shrinks the area.

Short tasks (5–10 minutes each). Every one has the same shape: change something, watch, then put it into words.

  • Move the numerator only. Keep the left at 2/3 and take the right from 3/5 to 4/5. Find where the larger one changes over and say it out loud.
  • Move the denominator only. Set the left to 1/2 and raise the denominator to 3, then 4. Confirm that a larger denominator means a smaller area, and separate that from "cut finer, so more".
  • Hunt for equal sizes. Find three pairs, such as 1/2 and 2/4 or 2/3 and 4/6, written differently but equal in area. Put the relationship between numerator and denominator into words.
  • Predict before cutting to match. Enter 5/8 and 2/3 and say which is larger before taking the matching step. Confirm that the eye cannot settle it (15/24 against 16/24).
  • Count the parts. Check that 3 and 5 become 15, and 4 and 6 become 12, then say why those numbers.
  • What this screen cannot check. Cakes of different sizes cannot be compared here — the frames are always equal. Discuss why they cannot.

What this screen covers

  • Only fractions between 0 and 1. The numerator cannot exceed the denominator. Improper fractions, mixed numbers and negative fractions are not included.
  • The size of the whole cannot be changed. Both frames are always equal, so comparisons between different wholes are out of scope here.
  • Denominators go up to 12. Finer than that and the difference in area stops being visible.
  • Nothing is reduced to lowest terms. 1/2 and 2/4 come out as the same area but keep their own notation; practising the reduction procedure is not included.
  • Addition, subtraction, multiplication and division are not included.
  • No check of learning effect has been carried out. What is published here is a draft of the material.
