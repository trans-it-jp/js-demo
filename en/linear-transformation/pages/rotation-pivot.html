Rotation about an arbitrary point

To rotate about a point c other than the origin: bring c to the origin, rotate, and put it back. All three matrices of T(c)·R(θ)·T(−c) are shown separately.

The question
In what order do the three matrices act on a point?
Assumed background
Coordinates, and multiplying tables of numbers (matrices). Written for upper-secondary level and above.
Draw Click or drag on the figure to draw. Anything you draw is transformed by the same matrix.

The dashed outline is the original shape and the solid one is the result. The pale grid shows where the cells go.

Things to check

  • Compare: move the centre back to the origin and confirm you get the same matrix as the previous lesson.
  • Explain: from the order of composition, say why T(−c) acts first.
  • Transfer: with the centre at (2, 1) and θ = 180 degrees, compute the image of the origin by hand and check the figure.
For teachers

The easy misreading: Thinking a rotation about an arbitrary point can be written with one rotation matrix. It takes three steps: move to the origin, rotate, move back.

A short task (5–10 minutes): Check that putting the centre c at the origin gives the same matrix as the previous lesson, then move c and watch only the third column of the combined matrix change.

What this page covers

  • Affine transforms only. The third row of the matrix is always [0, 0, 1]; projective transforms are not included.
  • Shapes can only be drawn with a pointer, not from the keyboard. Every parameter can also be set in a number field.
  • No check of learning effect has been carried out. This page is a draft of the material.
